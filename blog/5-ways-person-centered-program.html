5 Ways to Make Your Program More Person-Centered

Quick answer

A person-centered program starts with each person's goals and strengths. The fastest way to begin is to look at your intake forms, your success measures, and how you act on feedback, then make one small change to each. Every step below fits the budget you have and begins with a different first question.

Most disability organizations care deeply about the people they serve, and a few small shifts help that care show up in every form, measure, and meeting.

After more than 20 years helping organizations move toward a person-centered, rights-based model, these are the five practices I come back to most, each with a simple first step.

1.Start your intake with goals

The first questions on an intake form show a person what your program values most. When they ask about goals first, you invite the person to lead from the very first conversation.

Try this: Move one or two goal-oriented questions ("What does a good day look like for you?" or "What do you want more of in your life right now?") to the top of the form, ahead of the diagnostic and eligibility questions.

2.Measure what matters to each person

Attendance, incident reports, and hours of service tell part of the story. Adding a measure tied to a person's own goal shows whether your program is helping them get where they want to go.

Try this: Add one outcome measure tied to a person's own stated goal, such as "Did this person report feeling closer to their goal this quarter?" One meaningful measure goes a long way.

3.Make accommodation the starting point

A ramp, a large-print form, or an extra ten minutes all matter. Building them in from the beginning means the program is designed around the person from day one.

Try this: Before your next program redesign, invite a member of the disability community you serve to review the plan before it's finalized, so access is part of the design.

4.Help every staff member know what each person wants

When a frontline staff member can describe a person's goals in the person's own words, support becomes personal and consistent. This is a skill that grows with practice and a supportive culture.

Try this: Build "What does this person want, in their words?" into your regular case reviews and staff check-ins, alongside your written care plans.

5.Close the feedback loop

Satisfaction surveys and suggestion boxes gather valuable input. The next step is showing the people you serve what changed because they spoke up, and one concrete example from the past year builds trust quickly.

Try this: Pick one piece of recent feedback you've already received and share what changed and why, even if the change is small.

These steps fit the budget you have

Every step above is a shift in what you ask, measure, and prioritize, so you can begin with the resources you have today.

If more than one of these feels like a good fit for your organization, that is a great place to start. A focused look at your current intake, measures, and feedback loops can help you find the best place to begin.

Good questions

Frequently asked

Quick, clear answers about person-centered program development.

What does it mean for a disability program to be "person-centered"?

A person-centered program is built around an individual's strengths, goals, and rights first, and their diagnosis second. It starts from what matters to the person and builds support around that.

Can a well-intentioned program become more inclusive?

Yes. Programs run by caring, well-meaning staff can grow in inclusion by updating habits and structures such as intake forms, measures, and feedback loops.

How do I start making my organization's programs more person-centered?

Start small: change one intake question to ask about goals before diagnosis, or add one outcome measure that reflects what the person actually wanted. A disability consultant can help you review current programs and choose the highest-impact changes first.
