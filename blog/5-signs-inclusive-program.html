5 Signs Your Program Isn't as Inclusive as You Think

Quick answer

A program can look inclusive on paper and still be built around managing a diagnosis instead of supporting a person. The fastest way to tell the difference: look at your intake forms, your success metrics, and what changes when someone actually complains. If those are still built around deficits and compliance, the fixes below are usually cheap and don't require a bigger budget, just a different starting question.

Most disability organizations don't set out to be deficit-based. Somewhere between the intake form, the funding requirements, and the day-to-day pace of the work, programs start being built around what's easiest to document rather than what actually matters to the person in front of you.

After more than a decade helping organizations move toward a person-centered, rights-based model, these are the five signs I see most often, and the small, doable shift that tends to fix each one.

1.Your intake forms ask about diagnosis before they ask about goals

If the first three questions on your intake paperwork are about diagnosis, functioning level, or what someone "can't" do, you've already told that person what your program thinks is most important about them, before you've asked a single thing about what they actually want.

The fix: Move one or two goal-oriented questions ("What does a good day look like for you?" or "What do you want more of in your life right now?") to the top of the form, ahead of the diagnostic and eligibility questions.

2.Success is measured by compliance, not outcomes for the actual person

If your program's dashboards track attendance, incident reports, and hours of service delivered, but nothing about whether the person is closer to what they said they wanted, you're measuring the program's convenience, not its impact.

The fix: Add just one outcome measure tied to a person's own stated goal, even something as simple as "did this person report feeling closer to their goal this quarter." One good measure beats ten easy ones.

3."Accommodation" is the finish line, not the starting point

Meeting the legal minimum, a ramp, a large-print form, an extra ten minutes, is necessary, but it's not the same as designing the program around the person from the beginning. When accommodation is treated as the finish line, it usually means the program was designed for someone else first.

The fix: Before your next program redesign, ask a member of the disability community you serve to review the plan before it's finalized, not after it's already built and you're retrofitting access in.

4.Staff can recite policy but can't tell you what an individual actually wants

Ask a frontline staff member to describe a specific person's goals, in the person's own words, and if they hesitate or default to describing the person's diagnosis instead, that's a training gap and a culture gap, not a paperwork gap.

The fix: Build "what does this person want, in their words" into your regular case reviews and staff check-ins, not just your written care plans that get filed away and rarely reopened.

5.Feedback from the people you serve doesn't change anything

Satisfaction surveys and suggestion boxes are common. Programs that actually change something in response to what they hear are rarer. If you can't point to one concrete change your program made in the last year because of participant or family feedback, the feedback loop isn't really open.

The fix: Pick one piece of recent feedback you've already received and close the loop publicly, tell the people you serve what changed and why, even if the change is small.

None of this requires a bigger budget

Every fix above is a shift in what you ask, measure, and prioritize, not a new line item. That's the heart of person-centered program development: building around the individual first is usually more cost-effective over time, not less, because programs that fit the person tend to need less correcting later.

If you recognized your organization in more than one of these signs, that's normal, and it's also a good place to start. A focused audit of your current intake, metrics, and feedback loops is often enough to find the highest-impact, lowest-cost place to begin.

Good questions

Frequently asked

Quick, clear answers about person-centered program development.

What does it mean for a disability program to be "person-centered" instead of "deficit-based"?

A person-centered program is built around an individual's strengths, goals, and rights first, and their diagnosis second. A deficit-based program starts from what someone can't do and builds services to manage that, which tends to produce compliance rather than real inclusion.

Can a program be well-intentioned and still not be very inclusive?

Yes. Most of the signs in this article show up in programs run by caring, well-meaning staff. Inclusion problems are usually structural, built into intake forms, metrics, and habits, rather than a matter of bad intent.

How do I start making my organization's programs more person-centered?

Start small: change one intake question to ask about goals before diagnosis, or add one outcome measure that reflects what the person actually wanted. A disability consultant can help you audit current programs and prioritize the highest-impact, lowest-cost changes first.
